Formulas

  • v = a·î + b·ĵ
  • î = (1, 0)
  • ĵ = (0, 1)
  • |v| = √(a² + b²)

î ("i-hat") and ĵ ("j-hat") are the standard basis vectors — î points exactly one unit along the x-axis, ĵ points exactly one unit along the y-axis. The "hat" symbol means a vector with length exactly 1 (a unit vector).
a·î + b·ĵ ("a linear combination") means: stretch î by a, stretch ĵ by b, then place them tip-to-tail — where you land is v.
(a, b) is shorthand for that same vector, written as coordinates instead of a sum.
|v| means the length (magnitude) of v, found the same way as a diagonal — by the Pythagorean theorem.

What basis vectors mean

v = 2·î + 1·ĵ = (2, 1)

î and ĵ are called a basis because every vector on this plane — no matter where it points — can be built from just these two, each scaled by some amount. That's the whole idea behind coordinates: "(2, 1)" is really just a compact way of saying "2 steps along î, 1 step along ĵ." This same idea extends beyond 2D — 3D space uses three basis vectors, and higher dimensions use more.
